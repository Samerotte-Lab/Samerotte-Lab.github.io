Proteins must fold into the right shapes to function, and mutations that disrupt folding are often assumed to harm cells by producing toxic misfolded proteins. But how costly is protein misfolding, and why are some misfolded proteins so much more harmful than others? Our early work demonstrated that even small amounts of misfolded protein can reduce cellular fitness, independently of any loss of protein function. This suggested that the production of toxic misfolded proteins could be an important constraint on molecular evolution.

More recently, however, we discovered something surprising: **the mutations predicted to destabilize proteins the most are not necessarily the ones that impose the greatest fitness costs.** By measuring the effects of thousands of protein variants, we found that protein destabilization often predicts the cost of losing protein function, but not the collateral costs of producing altered proteins. These findings challenge the assumption that misfolding is a universal source of cellular toxicity. Our lab is now interested in understanding what makes particular protein variants harmful, how cells respond to damaged or misfolded proteins, and what these relationships reveal about the molecular constraints shaping evolution.
